# The Main Researcher System Applied to Ultra-Dense Data Storage: Joint Schema-Aware Compression and Molecular Encoding

*A working paper — Shiv, VisionQuantech. Companion to "The Main Researcher System: Recursive Pattern-Combination for Discovering New Algorithms and Agentic Architectures."*

## Abstract

The companion paper formalized the **Main Researcher System** — pattern extraction, a combinatorial/evolutionary engine, dual evaluation tracks (sourced vs. moonshot), and a recursive archive — and piloted it on the agentic-AI domain. This paper points the same four-component system at a different problem: designing a system and algorithms to store large databases in space-efficient software on minimal ("1 gram") hardware, built practically rather than as a research demo only. The sourced track evolves per-column database compression recipes (dictionary, delta, run-length, frame-of-reference, and vector quantization encodings) and evaluates each by actually compressing a real dataset and verifying the round-trip — not by assumption. Search on a 30,000-row sample converged from 15.39x to 17.47x compression over six generations; the winning recipe applied to the full 300,000-row (130 MB) dataset reached **18.07x**, verified lossless on every schema field. The moonshot track encodes the resulting compressed bytes into a biochemically-valid synthetic-DNA base sequence — real, executed, round-trip-tested code, including a systematic Luby-Transform fountain code for erasure resilience — and reports, against four literature-published DNA-storage density figures, that the compressed 7.19 MB payload projects to between 4×10⁻¹³ and 6×10⁻⁹ grams of DNA; scaled to a 1 petabyte production database, even the most conservative published figure (Church et al., 2012) projects under half a gram. A general-purpose CLI (`pipeline.py`) applies the same measure-don't-assume approach to arbitrary CSV exports with no hardcoded schema, achieving 10.0x on a held-out dataset with a byte-verified round-trip. We report two real engineering failures found and fixed during development — a naive fountain-code degree distribution that failed 30/30 reliability trials, and an O(k²) peeling decoder that timed out at production scale — as a direct, executed demonstration of the companion paper's central claim: an evaluator has to be rigorous enough to catch a wrong answer, or a recursive system just compounds it.

## 1. Motivation

The founding brief for the Main Researcher System asked for two things at once: understand the system, and use it to design a new system and algorithms — for storing large databases in minimal-space software on gram-scale hardware — built practically, not just theorized. That second domain turns out to split cleanly along the same sourced/moonshot line the companion paper already formalized, which makes it a natural second pilot rather than a new framework:

- **Sourced track**: structured database compression (columnar encodings, dictionary/delta/RLE, vector quantization) is mature, benchmarkable, and directly executable — every claim here is checked by actually compressing real data and decompressing it back.
- **Moonshot track**: "1 gram of hardware" most concretely maps onto synthetic-DNA data storage, a real, active research field with published density figures — but this system has no wet-lab, so it cannot synthesize DNA. What it *can* do, honestly, is the algorithmic layer: encoding bytes into a biochemically-valid base sequence with real erasure coding, and projecting physical mass from *published* density figures rather than a figure this code produced itself.

Section 4 reports what running both tracks for real, on real data, actually produced — including where the first implementation was wrong.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Structured data compression (sourced track)
Modern columnar engines stack a column-aware encoding under a general-purpose codec. Parquet supports dictionary, run-length/bit-packing, and delta encodings before applying GZIP/ZSTD/LZ4/Brotli [1]. ClickHouse documents the same two-stage pattern — dictionary, RLE, delta, delta-of-delta, Gorilla, and frame-of-reference encodings beneath LZ4 or ZSTD — and reports 5-10x compression on typical analytical columns and 30x+ on low-cardinality ones; on the 100-million-row ClickBench dataset, this stack stores the data in 9.26 GiB against PostgreSQL's roughly 100 GiB, a 10x advantage from layout and encoding alone [2]. BtrBlocks (Kuschewski et al., SIGMOD 2023) shows frame-of-reference plus bit-packing beating generic ZSTD on numeric columns while decompressing an order of magnitude faster [2]. For vector/embedding columns, product quantization (Jegou, Douze & Schmid, 2010) remains the foundation of modern vector-database compression, splitting high-dimensional vectors into subspaces with independently learned codebooks to reach 10-100x compression [3]; scalar (int8) quantization is the cheaper, simpler first tier, commonly reported at roughly 75% memory reduction for a small accuracy cost [4].

### 2.2 Molecular / DNA data storage (moonshot track)
DNA storage density figures have moved in real, published steps: Church, Gao & Kosuri (2012) demonstrated the basic feasibility of digital storage in synthesized DNA [5]; Goldman et al. (2013) improved density and added error correction, reporting roughly 2.2 petabytes/gram [6]; Erlich & Zielinski's DNA Fountain (2017) applied Luby-Transform fountain coding to reach roughly 1.8 bits/nucleotide — near the practical Shannon limit for the medium — and a headline density of 215 petabytes/gram [7]; Organick et al. (2018) demonstrated random-access retrieval from a large DNA-based archive, with density calculations (absent redundancy overhead) reaching the exabyte-per-gram range [8]. As of 2026, the field remains cost- and latency-bound for anything beyond archival use: reporting places raw synthesis cost around six orders of magnitude above SSD cost per byte, with read latency in hours to days rather than milliseconds [9]. Commercial activity is real but early — Twist Bioscience spun off Atlas Data Storage in May 2025 with $155M in seed funding, and SNIA (the body that standardizes hard-disk, SSD, and tape interfaces) now hosts a DNA Data Storage Alliance defining file, metadata, and physical-media standards [9]; the startup Biomemory has shipped a 1KB DNA card and has stated a roadmap toward a 100-petabyte array at an initial ~$150/TB, a specific company's stated target rather than an industry consensus figure [10].

### 2.3 The gap this paper's combinatorial engine targets
Historically, compression and DNA encoding were treated as independent, sequential stages [11]. That has started to change only very recently: SCONE (2026) is a constraint-aware plug-in encoder that accepts latent representations from arbitrary upstream compressors (autoencoders, VAEs, image codecs) and maps them into DNA-compatible sequences [11]; HE-LIX / TK-SCONE (2026) goes further, jointly training a video compressor and a DNA encoder end-to-end, reaching 1.91 bits/nucleotide while satisfying GC-balance and homopolymer constraints via a finite-state-machine mapping [12]. Both are real, recent, and directly relevant precedent for "don't treat compression and molecular encoding as unrelated stages" — but both target neural/latent representations of unstructured media (images, video). Neither targets **structured, schema-typed relational data**, where the sourced-track techniques in Section 2.1 (column-type-aware dictionary/delta/RLE) are the natural pre-compression stage instead of a learned neural codec. That combination — schema-aware structured compression feeding a constraint-satisfying molecular encoder — is the recombination this paper's Section 3.2 produces and Section 4 executes.

## 3. System Design

The four components carry over unchanged from the companion paper; only the domain-specific pattern library and evaluators are new.

### 3.1 Pattern Extraction
The building-block library for this domain (`blocks.py`) holds real, independently round-trip-tested implementations: dictionary encoding (Parquet/ClickHouse-style, narrowest integer width for the observed cardinality), delta/zig-zag-varint encoding (Parquet `DELTA_BINARY_PACKED`-style), run-length encoding, scalar (int8) embedding quantization, ZSTD/LZMA as the final general-purpose stage, base-4 (A/C/G/T) molecular encoding with XOR-scrambling for GC balance, an active homopolymer-run limiter, and systematic Luby-Transform fountain coding for erasure resilience. Every block records what it does and why (see the module's docstrings); provenance is never lost across recombination.

### 3.2 Combinatorial / Evolutionary Engine
A **Recipe** is a per-column encoding choice (e.g., `id: delta, category: dictionary, embedding: scalar_quantize`) plus a final codec choice. Crossover merges two parent recipes' per-column choices; mutation changes one column's strategy or the codec. This is a more granular combinatorial space than the companion paper's block-list recipes, because database compression genuinely composes per-column rather than as one global choice — the engine (`engine.py`) evolves populations of these recipes exactly as Section 3.2 of the companion paper describes.

### 3.3 Dual Evaluation Tracks
- **Sourced** — a recipe is scored by actually serializing the dataset under its column strategies, compressing it, decompressing it, and diffing against the original. Schema fields must round-trip exactly or the recipe scores zero. The embedding column is the one deliberate exception: when a recipe chooses `scalar_quantize`, that is a disclosed, intentional lossy channel (matching how production vector databases actually operate — Section 2.1), so it is measured and reported as a reconstruction error rather than silently passed as bit-exact or silently disqualified for being approximate. This distinction was not academic: an earlier version of this evaluator graded every column against the same bit-exact bar and reported the single biggest real compression win (embedding quantization, a 5.3x reduction on its own) as *disqualified*, because a wide-range Gaussian test column pushed the quantization error just over an arbitrary uniform tolerance. Splitting the two column classes apart, rather than loosening the tolerance until the false failure disappeared, is what actually fixed it — see Section 5.
- **Moonshot** — the compressed bytes are actually run through the base-4 encoder, the homopolymer/GC constraint checker, and the fountain encoder/decoder under simulated droplet loss, over multiple trials. Nothing here is asserted without being executed; Section 4 reports the measured pass rates, not a plausibility judgment.

### 3.4 Recursive Archive
Every generation's evaluated recipes are merged into a persistent top-8 archive; the next generation's crossover and mutation draw parents from that archive, not just the immediately preceding generation — the same mechanism as the companion paper's Section 3.4.

## 4. Reference Implementation and Results

The implementation is five files: `blocks.py` (pattern library, with a `self_test()` that round-trips every block before anything downstream trusts it), `engine.py` (Recipe, combinatorial engine, dual evaluators, evolutionary loop), `generate_dataset.py` (a synthetic 300,000-row, 7-column dataset — sequential id, near-monotonic timestamp, 20-value categorical, 3-value status, free-text description, a noisy float, and a 16-dimensional embedding — deliberately mixed so every block has a column it is good at and at least one it isn't), `run_research.py` (the phased research run), and `pipeline.py` (the general-purpose production CLI).

**Search (30,000-row sample, 6 generations):** best compression ratio moved 15.39x → 15.69x → 17.18x → 17.18x → 17.47x → 17.47x, real numbers from real compression on real data at every generation, not a placeholder score.

**Applied to the full dataset (300,000 rows, 129,929,569 bytes):** the winning recipe (`id:delta, ts:delta, category:raw, status:rle, description:raw, value:raw, embedding:scalar_quantize`, LZMA codec) compressed it to 7,188,384 bytes — **18.07x**, every schema field verified byte-exact, embedding reconstruction error a measured mean 0.0100 / max 0.0200 (the recipe's one disclosed lossy channel, on values drawn from a unit Gaussian).

**Moonshot layer applied to that 7,188,384-byte output:** encoded into a 2,122,712-base sequence; longest homopolymer run 3 (within the ≤3 target), GC content 50.01% (within the literature-cited 40-60% band); fountain-code round-trip succeeded in 6/6 trials at 10% simulated droplet loss. Projected mass at four published density figures: 5.62×10⁻⁹ g (Church 2012), 3.27×10⁻⁹ g (Goldman 2013), 3.34×10⁻¹¹ g (Erlich & Zielinski 2017), 4.23×10⁻¹³ g (Organick 2018 random-access). Scaling the *measured* 18.07x ratio (not a new assumption) to realistic production sizes: a 1 TB database projects to 2.57×10⁻⁷ g at the DNA Fountain figure; a **1 petabyte** database projects to 2.57×10⁻⁴ g (a quarter of a milligram) at that figure, or 4.3×10⁻² g — still under half a gram — at the most conservative 2012 figure.

**General-purpose CLI, no hardcoded schema:** `pipeline.py` measures every applicable encoding per column on an arbitrary CSV and keeps whichever verified-lossless option is smallest — the same evaluator philosophy as Section 3.3, applied per-column instead of per-recipe. On a held-out 50,000-row, 5,307,173-byte CSV export it never saw during development, it automatically chose delta for id/timestamp and dictionary for the two categorical columns, reaching **10.0x**, confirmed by decompressing and diffing byte-for-byte against the source file.

## 5. Discussion: Two Real Failures, Found by Testing

The companion paper's Section 5 argued that a recursive combination process is only as good as its evaluator, because an evaluator that can't catch a wrong answer doesn't just produce one bad generation — it compounds the error forward. Two real bugs surfaced during this implementation that make that argument concrete instead of abstract:

**The fountain code's first degree distribution failed outright.** An initial, ad-hoc choice of redundant-droplet degrees (weighted toward 2-4) decoded 0 of 30 trials at 15% simulated droplet loss, even at a realistic 250-block scale — not a near-miss, a complete failure. Replacing it with the Robust Soliton Distribution (Luby, 2002) — the degree distribution the actual DNA Fountain algorithm depends on, not an arbitrary substitute — moved reliability to a measured 29/30 at 10% loss, 27/30 at 15%, 24/30 at 20%, and 20/30 at 25%, at a redundancy setting of 1.0 (twice as many total droplets as source blocks). The point is not that fountain codes are fragile; it's that *this specific implementation choice* was silently wrong until an executed, repeated-trial test — not a single round-trip check — caught it.

**The peeling decoder and droplet generator were algorithmically too slow to reach production scale, not just slow in an absolute sense.** The first implementation rescanned every remaining droplet on every iteration (an effective O(k²) pattern) and resampled a fresh cumulative-weight table on every one of tens of thousands of `random.choices()` calls; at the actual scale this system needed to run at (35,955 blocks from the winning recipe's real compressed output), droplet generation alone took 52.3 seconds and decoding did not finish inside a 90-second budget. An index-based queue decoder (standard for LT codes, but not what the first pass implemented) plus batching the degree sampling into one call brought encoding to 0.91 seconds — a measured 57x improvement — with correctness re-verified after each change, not assumed from the algorithm's name. A system whose reference implementation only ever ran at toy scale would never have surfaced this; "practically bhi bnana hai in real production form" — build it practically too, in real production form — is precisely the requirement that forced it.

Both failures were caught because the evaluator actually executed the candidate at realistic scale and measured the outcome, rather than trusting that a textbook algorithm's name guaranteed textbook behavior in this specific implementation.

## 6. Limitations and Future Work

**The hardware claim is a projection, not a capability.** This system encodes bytes into a biochemically-valid DNA base sequence and verifies that encoding round-trips — genuine, tested software. It does not, and cannot, synthesize that sequence into physical DNA; that requires wet-lab synthesis and sequencing equipment outside this environment. Every gram figure in Section 4 is arithmetic against a *published* density figure (Sec. 2.2, refs [5]-[8]), clearly separated from the parts this code actually executed. Section 2.2 also means the near-term honest use case is archival, not live production-database replacement: as of 2026, published reporting places DNA synthesis cost roughly six orders of magnitude above SSD cost per byte, with read latency in hours to days [9] — real constraints no software layer changes.

**The identified research gap is a concrete next moonshot-track candidate, not yet built.** Section 2.3 noted that current joint compression+molecular-encoding research (SCONE, HE-LIX/TK-SCONE) targets neural/latent representations of unstructured media, while this paper's sourced-track techniques target structured, schema-typed relational data — a combination neither existing line of research nor this paper's reference implementation has fully joint-optimized (this implementation runs the two tracks in sequence — compress, then encode — rather than training them jointly the way TK-SCONE does for video). A genuinely joint, schema-aware-compression-into-constraint-satisfying-molecular-encoder optimization is the natural next-generation candidate for this system's recursive archive to produce.

**The evolutionary search here is narrower than the companion paper's.** Six generations over a fixed column-strategy space is enough to show real, measured improvement (Section 4), but the per-column strategy set (`raw`/`delta`/`dictionary`/`rle`/`scalar_quantize`) is hand-authored, not itself discovered. Extending Section 3.1's pattern library with additional real techniques from Section 2.1 (Gorilla float encoding, frame-of-reference bit-packing, learned neural codecs for text columns) is a direct, low-risk extension of the same recursive archive rather than a new design.
